# The Ring of Gyges IV: Whether Seen or Unseen

Nov 17, 2026 · @J

## Characters

- **JULIAN** — the young digital justice activist from the lounge, invisible guest in ancient Athens
- **SOCRATES** — the Athenian philosopher, at home in his own century
- **GLAUCON** — Plato's elder brother, young, ambitious, fearless in argument
- **ADEIMANTUS** — Glaucon's brother, graver and more searching
- **CEPHALUS** — a very old, wealthy shield-maker, host of the evening
- **POLEMARCHUS** — his son and heir
- **THRASYMACHUS** — a famous orator from Chalcedon
- **ARIA** and **CLEON** — Julian's friends, in the lounge
- Silent at Cephalus's house: LYSIAS and EUTHYDEMUS (Cephalus's sons), NICERATUS, CHARMANTIDES, CLEITOPHON

The Athenians speak Plato's words, from Benjamin Jowett's public-domain translation of the *Republic*, cut and condensed for the screen. Julian's lines and the final scenes between Julian and Socrates are new.

## Sequence One: The Brass Ring

FADE IN:

**INT. BOTANICAL LOUNGE – LOS ANGELES – DAWN**

The holographic screens are dark. Pale gold light leaks through the blinds onto the velvet booth. ARIA and CLEON gather their slates and shrug on their jackets. JULIAN stays seated, staring at the empty air where Socrates stood a moment ago.

On the table, exactly there, something glints: a plain, unadorned brass ring.

**CLEON**

He left it behind.

**ARIA** (softly)

He never leaves anything behind. Get some sleep, Julian.

Aria and Cleon go. The door sighs shut. Julian picks up the ring. It is warm, as if it had just left a hand. He slips it onto his right index finger, where Socrates wore it, and slowly turns it.

The smell of smog and botanical vapor is pierced by sun-baked stone, dried thyme and distant saltwater. The air around Julian begins to shimmer like a mirage. A rush of wind, and the lounge is gone.

DISSOLVE TO:

**EXT. THE PIRAEUS – HARBOUR OF ATHENS – DAY**

SUPER: The Piraeus, harbour of Athens. The festival of the goddess Bendis.

Blinding sun on white stone. Masts crowd the harbour. Drums and reed pipes: a procession of Thracians in bright cloaks and fox-skin caps winds down the street behind the image of their goddess.

Julian stands in the middle of it in his vest and city shoes. A fish-seller stares. A child points at his shoes. Two Scythian archers, the city's police, turn toward him.

**SOCRATES (V.O.)**

I went down yesterday to the Piraeus with Glaucon the son of Ariston, that I might offer up my prayers to the goddess; and also because I wanted to see in what manner they would celebrate the festival, which was a new thing.

Julian backs into a narrow alley. His fingers twist the ring, and the bezel slides round to the inside of his palm. The archers round the corner, look straight through him, and walk on.

Julian raises his hand in front of his face. He cannot see it. He turns the bezel outward: the hand flickers back. Inward: gone.

From here on, whenever the bezel is turned inward, we see Julian as a faint, glassy outline. The Athenians see nothing.

**JULIAN (V.O.)**

The Ring of Gyges. We spent two years calling it a metaphor.

Across the square, beside a tall young man, GLAUCON, walks a barefoot figure in a worn cloak: SOCRATES. Julian starts toward him, then stops himself.

Socrates halts mid-stride and frowns toward the alley, as though he has heard something. Before he can look closer, a SERVANT BOY catches him by the cloak from behind.

**SERVANT BOY**

Polemarchus desires you to wait.

POLEMARCHUS pushes through the crowd, with ADEIMANTUS, Glaucon's brother, NICERATUS and several other young men.

**POLEMARCHUS**

I perceive, Socrates, that you and your companion are already on your way to the city. But do you see how many we are? And are you stronger than all these? For if not, you will have to remain where you are.

**SOCRATES** (amused)

May there not be the alternative, that we may persuade you to let us go?

**POLEMARCHUS**

But can you persuade us, if we refuse to listen to you?

**GLAUCON**

Certainly not.

**ADEIMANTUS**

Has no one told you of the torch-race on horseback in honour of the goddess, which will take place in the evening?

**SOCRATES**

With horses! That is a novelty.

**POLEMARCHUS**

And a festival at night, which you certainly ought to see. There will be a gathering of young men, and we will have a good talk. Stay then, and do not be perverse.

**GLAUCON**

I suppose, since you insist, that we must.

The group sets off up the hill. Socrates glances back once at the empty alley, then follows. Julian, unseen, follows him.

CUT TO:

**INT. HOUSE OF CEPHALUS – COURTYARD – LATE AFTERNOON**

A courtyard open to the sky. Smoke rises from an altar where a sacrifice has just been made. Chairs and couches stand in a semicircle beneath painted columns. Racks of bronze shields, the family's trade, gleam along one wall.

In a cushioned chair sits CEPHALUS, very old, with a garland on his head. His sons LYSIAS and EUTHYDEMUS sit near him; apart from them, sprawled on a couch, is THRASYMACHUS of Chalcedon, with CLEITOPHON and CHARMANTIDES.

Julian slips in behind the last of the young men before the door swings shut, and flattens himself against a column.

**CEPHALUS** (saluting Socrates eagerly)

You don't come to see me, Socrates, as often as you ought. At my age I can hardly get to the city, and therefore you should come oftener to the Piraeus. For the more the pleasures of the body fade away, the greater to me is the pleasure and charm of conversation.

**SOCRATES** (sitting beside him)

There is nothing I like better, Cephalus, than conversing with aged men; for I regard them as travellers who have gone a journey which I too may have to go. Tell me: is life harder towards the end?

**CEPHALUS**

Many of my age lament the pleasures of youth. But how well I remember the aged poet Sophocles, when someone asked him, "How does love suit with age, Sophocles?" "Peace," he replied. "Most gladly have I escaped the thing of which you speak. I feel as if I had escaped from a mad and furious master."

Cephalus laughs, a dry, warm sound.

**CEPHALUS (CONT'D)**

The truth is, Socrates, that these complaints are to be attributed not to old age, but to men's characters and tempers.

**SOCRATES**

People will say that old age sits lightly upon you because you are rich, and wealth is a great comforter. What is the greatest blessing you have reaped from your wealth?

**CEPHALUS** (his smile fading)

When a man thinks himself to be near death, fears and cares enter into his mind which he never had before. The tales of a world below, and the punishment exacted there for deeds done here, were once a laughing matter to him. But now he is tormented with the thought that they may be true. He begins to reflect on what wrongs he has done to others, and many a time, like a child, he starts up in his sleep for fear.

The great blessing of riches, to a good man, is that he has had no occasion to deceive or to defraud others, and when he departs to the world below, he owes no offerings to the gods and no debts to men.

**SOCRATES**

Well said, Cephalus. But as concerning justice, what is it? To speak the truth and to pay your debts, no more than this? Suppose a friend, when in his right mind, has deposited arms with me, and he asks for them when he is not in his right mind. Ought I to give them back to him?

**CEPHALUS**

You are quite right; no one would say so.

**POLEMARCHUS** (interposing)

But it is the right definition, Socrates, if Simonides is to be believed.

**CEPHALUS** (rising slowly)

I fear I must go now, for I have to look after the sacrifices. I hand over the argument to Polemarchus.

**SOCRATES**

Is not Polemarchus your heir?

**CEPHALUS**

To be sure.

He goes out laughing to the altar. Polemarchus takes his father's chair.

**SOCRATES**

Tell me then, O heir of the argument, what did Simonides say about justice?

**POLEMARCHUS**

That it is just to give to each what is owed to him. A friend ought always to do good to a friend, and never evil. And to an enemy is owed what is proper to him, which is evil. Justice is doing good to your friends and harm to your enemies.

Against his column, Julian goes very still.

**JULIAN (V.O.)**

That was us. That was the whole Mirror Force. Unmask the enemy. Protect our own.

**SOCRATES**

But ought the just to injure any one at all?

**POLEMARCHUS**

Undoubtedly he ought to injure those who are both wicked and his enemies.

**SOCRATES**

When horses are injured, are they improved or deteriorated?

**POLEMARCHUS**

Deteriorated.

**SOCRATES**

And will not men who are injured be deteriorated in that which is the proper virtue of man? And that human virtue is justice? Then men who are injured are of necessity made more unjust. But can the musician by his art make men unmusical? Can the just by justice make men unjust? Can the good harm any one, any more than heat can produce cold?

**POLEMARCHUS**

It cannot.

**SOCRATES**

Then to injure a friend or any one else is not the act of a just man, but of the opposite, who is the unjust.

**POLEMARCHUS** (after a long pause)

I think that what you say is quite true, Socrates. I am quite ready to do battle at your side against any one who says otherwise.

Julian looks down at the empty space where his hand should be.

On his couch, THRASYMACHUS has been trying to break in for some time. Now he gathers himself up and comes at them like a wild beast.

## Sequence Two: The Interest of the Stronger

**INT. HOUSE OF CEPHALUS – COURTYARD – CONTINUOUS**

THRASYMACHUS plants himself in the middle of the semicircle. The young men shrink back on their couches. Even Julian, behind his column, flinches.

**THRASYMACHUS** (roaring to the whole company)

What folly, Socrates, has taken possession of you all? And why do you knock under to one another? If you want really to know what justice is, you should not only ask but answer. And I will not have you say that justice is duty or advantage or profit or gain or interest, for this sort of nonsense will not do for me. I must have clearness and accuracy.

**SOCRATES** (with a quiver in his voice)

Thrasymachus, don't be hard upon us. If we were seeking a piece of gold, you would not imagine that we were knocking under to one another. And we are seeking justice, a thing more precious than many pieces of gold. Answer us yourself.

**THRASYMACHUS**

Listen, then. I proclaim that justice is nothing else than the interest of the stronger. (a beat) And now why do you not praise me? But of course you won't.

**SOCRATES**

Let me first understand you.

**THRASYMACHUS**

The different forms of government make laws, democratical, aristocratical, tyrannical, with a view to their several interests. And these laws, made by them for their own interests, are the justice which they deliver to their subjects. Him who transgresses them they punish as a breaker of the law, and unjust. Everywhere there is one principle of justice, which is the interest of the stronger.

INSERT – THE LAWS OF THE STRONG: three thrones (a tyrant, a council of the rich, an assembly of the many). From each, laws stream down onto the subjects below, and each stream bends back up toward the throne that wrote it.

**JULIAN (V.O.)**

Thrasymachian realism. Socrates said those words in our booth. I never thought I'd watch the man himself say it.

**SOCRATES**

But tell me, Thrasymachus: is the physician, in the strict sense, a money-maker or a healer of the sick? And the true pilot: is he a mere sailor, or a ruler of sailors? Then no physician, in so far as he is a physician, considers his own good in what he prescribes, but the good of his patient. And the pilot provides for the interest of the sailor who is under him, not for his own. Then no science or art considers or enjoins the interest of the stronger, but only the interest of the subject and weaker.

Thrasymachus's face darkens.

**THRASYMACHUS**

Tell me, Socrates, have you got a nurse?

**SOCRATES**

Why do you ask such a question, when you ought rather to be answering?

**THRASYMACHUS**

Because she leaves you to snivel, and never wipes your nose. She has not even taught you to know the shepherd from the sheep. You fancy that the shepherd fattens the sheep with a view to their own good, and not to the good of himself or his master. And you imagine that the rulers of states never think of their subjects as sheep, and that they are not studying their own advantage day and night.

He paces the courtyard, gathering force.

**THRASYMACHUS (CONT'D)**

Consider further, most foolish Socrates, that the just is always a loser in comparison with the unjust. When there is a tax, the just man will pay more and the unjust less on the same income. When the just man takes office, he neglects his own affairs, and he is hated by his friends for refusing to serve them in unlawful ways.

But look at the highest form of injustice: tyranny, which by fraud and force takes away the property of others, not little by little but wholesale. A man caught doing any one of these wrongs singly is called a thief and a swindler. But when he has taken the money of the citizens and made slaves of them as well, then instead of those names he is termed happy and blessed. For mankind censure injustice fearing that they may be the victims of it, and not because they shrink from committing it.

Silence. Thrasymachus gathers his cloak to leave, but the young men rise and block his way.

**JULIAN (V.O.)**

Every platform we ever audited would sign that speech. Steal small and you're a criminal. Steal everything at once and you're on a magazine cover.

**SOCRATES**

Thrasymachus, excellent man, are you going to run away before you have fairly taught or learned whether your words are true? Is the attempt to determine how life may be passed by each one of us to the greatest advantage so small a matter in your eyes?

Thrasymachus sits again, sullen.

**SOCRATES (CONT'D)**

Tell me: do you think that a city, or an army, or a band of robbers and thieves, or any other gang of evil-doers, could act at all if they injured one another?

**THRASYMACHUS**

No indeed, they could not.

**SOCRATES**

Because injustice creates divisions and hatreds and fighting, and justice imparts harmony and friendship. And wherever injustice takes up her abode, whether in a city, an army, a family, or in a single person, that body is rendered incapable of united action. It becomes its own enemy.

The heat of the day still hangs over the courtyard. Sweat runs down Thrasymachus's face. For a moment, Socrates watches something he has never seen before: Thrasymachus blushing.

**THRASYMACHUS** (curtly)

Let this, Socrates, be your entertainment at the festival of Bendis.

**SOCRATES**

For which I am indebted to you, now that you have grown gentle and left off scolding. Nevertheless, I have not been well entertained, and the fault is my own. As an epicure snatches a taste of every dish brought to table, so have I gone from one subject to another without discovering what I sought at first: the nature of justice.

(spreading his hands)

And the result of the whole discussion has been that I know nothing at all.

He sits back, as if the evening is over. In the corner, GLAUCON gets slowly to his feet.

## Sequence Three: The Ring of Gyges

**INT. HOUSE OF CEPHALUS – COURTYARD – DUSK**

Servants light the oil lamps. The sky above the courtyard deepens to violet. GLAUCON stands in the middle of the semicircle, where Thrasymachus stood.

**GLAUCON**

Socrates, do you wish really to persuade us, or only to seem to have persuaded us, that to be just is always better than to be unjust?

**SOCRATES**

I should wish really to persuade you, if I could.

**GLAUCON**

Then you certainly have not succeeded. There are goods we welcome for their own sakes, such as harmless pleasures. There are goods we desire both for themselves and for their results, such as knowledge, sight and health. And there are goods we would never choose for their own sakes, but only for their rewards, such as gymnastic and the physician's art. In which class would you place justice?

**SOCRATES**

In the highest class: among those goods which he who would be happy desires both for their own sake and for the sake of their results.

**GLAUCON**

The many do not think so. They say that to do injustice is, by nature, good, and to suffer injustice, evil. And so, when men have both done and suffered injustice, they agree among themselves to have neither. Hence there arise laws and mutual covenants. Justice is a compromise between the best of all, which is to do injustice and not be punished, and the worst of all, which is to suffer injustice without the power of retaliation. It is honoured only by reason of the inability of men to do injustice.

(he begins to walk the semicircle)

If we would see this plainly, let us give both the just and the unjust power to do what they will, and watch whither desire will lead them. Such a power as is said to have been possessed by Gyges, the ancestor of Croesus the Lydian.

Behind his column, Julian's thumb finds the ring on his finger.

INSERT – THE SHEPHERD'S RING: Glaucon's story becomes images. A great storm and an earthquake split the earth where a young shepherd is feeding his flock. In the chasm lies a hollow bronze horse with doors. Inside, a dead body of more than human stature, wearing nothing but a ring.

**GLAUCON (V.O.)**

This he took from the finger of the dead and reascended. The shepherds met together to send their monthly report about the flocks to the king. As he was sitting among them, he chanced to turn the collet of the ring inside his hand, when instantly he became invisible to the rest of the company, and they began to speak of him as if he were no longer present. Again touching the ring, he turned the collet outwards and reappeared.

The images continue: the shepherd at the royal court; the queen; the king slain in his bed; the shepherd crowned.

**GLAUCON (V.O.) (CONT'D)**

Whereupon he contrived to be chosen one of the messengers sent to the court. As soon as he arrived he seduced the queen, and with her help conspired against the king and slew him, and took the kingdom.

BACK TO SCENE.

**GLAUCON**

Suppose now that there were two such magic rings, and the just put on one of them and the unjust the other. No man can be imagined to be of such an iron nature that he would stand fast in justice. No man would keep his hands off what was not his own, when he could safely take what he liked out of the market, or go into houses, or kill or release from prison whom he would, and in all respects be like a god among men.

Then the actions of the just would be as the actions of the unjust. For wherever any one thinks that he can safely be unjust, there he is unjust.

As Glaucon speaks, Socrates's eyes drift past him, to the lamplit column where Julian stands. They rest there a moment too long.

**JULIAN (V.O.)**

Glaucon designed the experiment twenty-four centuries ago. I'm the one running it.

**GLAUCON**

And now, to judge the two lives truly, we must isolate them. Let the unjust man be perfectly unjust, and yet have the greatest reputation for justice. And let the just man be the best of men, simple and noble, and let him be thought the worst. They will tell you that the just man who is thought unjust will be scourged, racked, bound, will have his eyes burnt out, and at last, after suffering every kind of evil, he will be impaled. Then he will understand that he ought to seem only, and not to be, just.

**SOCRATES**

Heavens, my dear Glaucon! How energetically you polish them up for the decision, first one and then the other, as if they were two statues.

ADEIMANTUS rises beside his brother.

**ADEIMANTUS**

The strongest point has not even been mentioned, Socrates. Fathers tell their sons that they ought to be just, but they praise not justice itself, only the reputation it brings. Even the poets tell us the gods can be bought with sacrifices and soothing words.

So I say: not only prove to us that justice is better than injustice, but show what either of them does to the one who has it, which makes the one a good and the other an evil, whether seen or unseen by gods and men.

*Whether seen or unseen.* The words hang in the lamplight. Julian slowly lowers his hand.

**SOCRATES** (delighted, looking from one brother to the other)

Sons of an illustrious father! There is something truly divine in being able to argue as you have done for the superiority of injustice, and remaining unconvinced by your own arguments. I feel that I am unequal to the task. And yet I cannot refuse to help, while breath and speech remain to me. It would be an impiety to be present when justice is evil spoken of and not lift up a hand in her defence.

(settling back into his chair)

The enquiry is no easy one, and needs keen eyes. Suppose a short-sighted man were asked to read small letters from a distance, and someone told him the same letters were written larger in another place. He would read the larger first, and then the lesser. Justice is the virtue of a single man, and also of a whole city. And the city is the larger. Let us watch a city come into being in speech, and we shall see its justice and injustice come into being too.

**ADEIMANTUS**

That is an excellent proposal.

Julian slides down the column and sits on the cool stone floor, knees up, settling in for the night.

## Sequence Four: The City and the Soul

**INT. HOUSE OF CEPHALUS – COURTYARD – NIGHT**

Full dark above the courtyard. The lamps throw long shadows across the painted columns. Through the open gate, far down the street, horsemen gallop past carrying torches, passing the flames from hand to hand. The torch race. Nobody in the courtyard gets up to look. Only Julian watches it go by.

**SOCRATES**

A city comes into being because no one of us is self-sufficing; we all have many wants. The first and greatest is food; the second, a dwelling; the third, clothing. So we need a farmer, a builder, a weaver, a shoemaker. And all things are produced more plentifully and better when one man does one thing which is natural to him.

They will feast on loaves of barley and wheat, with salt and olives and cheese, figs and beans. And with such a diet they may be expected to live in peace and health to a good old age.

**GLAUCON**

Yes, Socrates, and if you were providing for a city of pigs, how else would you feed the beasts? Give them the ordinary conveniences of life: sofas, and tables, and sauces and sweets in the modern style.

Laughter around the semicircle.

**SOCRATES**

Now I understand. You would have me consider not only how a city, but how a luxurious city is created. In my opinion the true and healthy city is the one I have described. But in a fevered city we may be more likely to see how justice and injustice originate. Your city will want more land, and then it will go to war. And so it will need guardians.

The guardian must be like a well-bred dog: gentle to his own people, and fierce to strangers. And to know the one from the other, he must be a lover of learning: a philosopher.

**ADEIMANTUS**

And how will the citizens be persuaded to accept their places?

**SOCRATES** (hesitating)

With a tale, Adeimantus, a single royal lie. I will tell them: Citizens, you are brothers, yet God has framed you differently. Some of you have the power of command, and in these he has mingled gold. Others he has made of silver, to be auxiliaries. Others again, who are to be husbandmen and craftsmen, he has composed of brass and iron.

Julian glances down at the ring on his finger.

**JULIAN (V.O.)**

Brass. Of course it's brass.

INSERT – THE CITY IN SPEECH: a city drawn in light, building itself layer by layer. At its base, the brass and iron workers: farmers, builders, weavers. Above them, the silver guardians with their shields. At the top, the few of gold. Lines of light connect each class to the one work it does.

**SOCRATES**

Now, Glaucon, like huntsmen we should surround the cover, and look sharp that justice does not steal away. Here is no path, and the wood is dark and perplexing; still we must push on.

Wisdom we found in the rulers, courage in the soldiers, and temperance is the harmony running through all of them. And justice…

He stops, then laughs at himself.

**SOCRATES (CONT'D)**

Halloo! I begin to perceive a track. Truly, we are stupid fellows. At the beginning of our enquiry, ages ago, justice was tumbling out at our feet, and we never saw her. Like people who go about looking for what they have in their hands. Do you remember the principle we laid down at the foundation of the city, that one man should practise one thing only, the thing to which his nature is best adapted? Justice is this: to do one's own work, and not to be a busybody.

**GLAUCON**

And now we must find the same letters, written small, in the single man.

**SOCRATES**

Tell me: when a thirsty man holds back from drinking, is there not something in his soul bidding him drink, and something forbidding him? The one which reasons we may call the rational part. The other, with which he loves and hungers and thirsts, the irrational or appetitive. But there is a third.

There is a story I once heard and put faith in. Leontius, the son of Aglaion, coming up one day from the Piraeus under the north wall, saw some dead bodies lying at the place of execution. He felt a desire to see them, and also a dread and abhorrence of them. For a time he struggled and covered his eyes. But at length the desire got the better of him, and forcing his eyes open, he ran up to the bodies, saying: "Look, ye wretches, take your fill of the fair sight."

INSERT – LEONTIUS: a young man under a city wall, hands over his eyes, then running toward something we do not see. His own fist shakes at his own eyes.

**SOCRATES (CONT'D)**

The moral of the tale is that anger at times goes to war with desire, as though they were two distinct things. That is spirit, the third part, the natural ally of reason.

**JULIAN (V.O.)**

Aria could build that man a feed in an afternoon. Desire on one side, disgust on the other, and a scroll that never ends.

INSERT – THE SOUL WRIT SMALL: the same three tiers as the city, now inside a single human figure. Reason, gold, in the head. Spirit, silver, in the chest. Appetite, brass and iron, in the belly. When each keeps to its own work, the figure glows steadily. When appetite surges upward, the light shatters.

**SOCRATES**

Justice is concerned not with the outward man, but with the inward. The just man does not permit the several parts within him to interfere with one another. He sets in order his own inner life, and is his own master and his own law, and at peace with himself. He binds together the three principles within him, like the higher, lower and middle notes of a scale, and is no longer many, but has become one.

Then virtue is the health and beauty and well-being of the soul, and vice its disease and weakness and deformity. Our old question still waits: which is the more profitable, to be just, whether seen or unseen of gods and men, or to be unjust and unpunished?

**GLAUCON**

In my judgment, Socrates, the question has now become ridiculous. When the body is gone, life is no longer endurable, though pampered with all kinds of meats and drinks, and all wealth and all power. And shall we be told that when the very essence of the soul is corrupted, life is still worth having, if only a man be allowed to do whatever he likes?

On the stone floor, Julian turns the ring slowly round and round his finger. He does not turn it outward.

## Sequence Five: The Philosopher and the Sun

**INT. HOUSE OF CEPHALUS – COURTYARD – LATER THAT NIGHT**

The lamps have been refilled. Several of the young men lie back on their couches; one is asleep. POLEMARCHUS leans across to ADEIMANTUS, takes him by the shoulder of his cloak, and whispers. Only Julian, sitting close, hears it.

**POLEMARCHUS** (whispering)

Shall we let him off, or what shall we do?

**ADEIMANTUS** (aloud)

Certainly not.

**SOCRATES**

Who is it whom you are refusing to let off?

**ADEIMANTUS**

You. You have been building a city, and you skipped over the hardest part. How can such a city ever come to be?

**SOCRATES** (sighing)

You are bringing three great waves down upon me. The first: that women, with the same nature, shall do the same work as men, and be guardians too. The second: that the guardians shall own nothing and hold all things in common, as one family. And now the third and greatest wave is coming.

He rises and stands at the centre of the semicircle.

**SOCRATES (CONT'D)**

Until philosophers are kings, or the kings and princes of this world have the spirit and power of philosophy, and political greatness and wisdom meet in one, cities will never have rest from their evils, nor the human race, as I believe.

Silence. Then GLAUCON leans forward, half laughing, half alarmed.

**GLAUCON**

Socrates, what do you mean? Numerous persons, and very respectable persons too, will pull off their coats all in a moment, seize any weapon that comes to hand, and run at you with might and main.

**SOCRATES**

You got me into the scrape.

**ADEIMANTUS**

And there is worse. Those who study philosophy too long become strange monsters, and the best of them are useless to their cities.

**SOCRATES**

Then hear a parable. Imagine a ship, with a captain who is taller and stronger than any of the crew, but a little deaf, with poor sight, and his knowledge of navigation not much better. The sailors are quarrelling about the steering. Every one thinks he has a right to steer, though he has never learned the art, and will further assert that it cannot be taught.

INSERT – THE SHIP OF STATE: a great ship on a dark sea. Sailors crowd around a dazed captain, pull at the wheel, and pour wine down his throat. At the bow, alone, a figure studies the stars. The crew points at him and laughs.

**SOCRATES (V.O.)**

They chain up the noble captain's senses with drink or some narcotic drug, mutiny and take possession of the ship, and make free with the stores. The man who helps them seize it, they call a sailor, a pilot, an able seaman. And the true pilot, who pays attention to the seasons and sky and stars and winds, they call a prater, a star-gazer, a good-for-nothing.

**JULIAN (V.O.)**

Drug the captain. Flatter the crew. Laugh at the one who reads the stars. The feed runs that voyage every night.

BACK TO SCENE.

**ADEIMANTUS**

Then tell us at least what the philosopher steers by. What is the good?

**SOCRATES**

No, sweet sirs. To reach what is now in my thoughts would be an effort too great for me. But of the child of the good, who is likest him, I would fain speak.

He points up, past the edge of the roof, to where the sun set hours ago.

**SOCRATES (CONT'D)**

The eyes, when they are turned to objects on which the light of day is no longer shining, but the moon and stars only, see dimly and are nearly blind. But when they are turned to things on which the sun shines, they see clearly. The sun is not sight, but the author of sight. This is he whom I call the child of the good. As the sun is in the visible world, so is the good in the world of mind. It gives truth to the things known, and the power of knowing to the knower. And the good is not essence, but far exceeds essence in dignity and power.

**GLAUCON** (with ludicrous earnestness)

By the light of heaven, how amazing!

The young men roar with laughter. In the corner, Julian laughs too, a short snort he cannot stop. It cuts through the courtyard.

The laughter dies. Every head turns toward the empty column. POLEMARCHUS half rises.

**POLEMARCHUS**

Who was that?

Julian presses both hands over his mouth. Socrates looks directly at the empty column, a long, unreadable moment.

**SOCRATES** (mildly)

Perhaps the goddess, Polemarchus. It is her festival. Let us go on.

Polemarchus sits again, unconvinced.

INSERT – THE DIVIDED LINE: a single vertical line of light, cut into four. At the bottom, shadows and reflections. Above them, the things that cast them. Above that, the diagrams of the mathematician. At the top, the forms themselves, and over the whole line, the blazing sun of the good.

**SOCRATES**

And now, let me show in a figure how far our nature is enlightened or unenlightened.

The lamps gutter in a sudden draught. One by one, they go out.

CUT TO BLACK.

## Sequence Six: The Cave

**INT. HOUSE OF CEPHALUS – COURTYARD – DEEP NIGHT**

Darkness. A servant relights a single lamp on a stand behind the couches. At once the shadows of the whole company leap up onto the plastered wall in front of them, huge and wavering.

Julian stands among the shadows on the wall. His own is not there. He casts none.

**SOCRATES**

Behold! Human beings living in an underground den, which has a mouth open towards the light. Here they have been from their childhood, and have their legs and necks chained so that they cannot move, and can only see before them. Above and behind them a fire is blazing at a distance, and between the fire and the prisoners there is a raised way, and a low wall built along it, like the screen which marionette players have in front of them, over which they show the puppets.

INSERT – THE CAVE: a long underground den. Rows of prisoners chained by the legs and neck, faces to the rock wall. Behind them, a raised path and a low wall; behind that, a fire. Along the path, men carry statues and figures of animals above the wall, and their shadows slide across the rock in front of the prisoners.

**SOCRATES (V.O.)**

And do you see men passing along the wall carrying all sorts of vessels, and statues and figures of animals made of wood and stone? Some of them are talking, others silent.

**GLAUCON (V.O.)**

You have shown me a strange image, and they are strange prisoners.

**SOCRATES (V.O.)**

Like ourselves. They see only their own shadows, or the shadows of one another, which the fire throws on the opposite wall of the cave. And if the prison had an echo, would they not be sure to fancy, when one of the passers-by spoke, that the voice came from the passing shadow? To them, the truth would be literally nothing but the shadows of the images.

The prisoners call out to one another, naming the shadows. Some are crowned with laurel by their fellows.

**SOCRATES (V.O.) (CONT'D)**

And they confer honours among themselves on those who are quickest to observe the passing shadows, and to remark which went before and which followed after, and who are therefore best able to predict the future.

**JULIAN (V.O.)**

Puppeteers behind a screen. Shadows on a wall. Prizes for whoever predicts the next shadow best. We called it the digital cave and thought we'd invented the phrase.

**SOCRATES (V.O.)**

Now suppose one of them is released and compelled suddenly to stand up and turn his neck round and look towards the light. He will suffer sharp pains. The glare will distress him, and he will fancy that the shadows which he formerly saw are truer than the objects now shown to him.

One prisoner is freed. He staggers, shields his eyes from the fire, and is dragged up a steep, rugged passage toward a white opening.

**SOCRATES (V.O.) (CONT'D)**

And suppose he is reluctantly dragged up a steep and rugged ascent, and held fast until he is forced into the presence of the sun himself. When he approaches the light his eyes will be dazzled. First he will see the shadows best, next the reflections of men and other objects in the water, and then the objects themselves. Then the moon and the stars and the spangled heaven. Last of all he will be able to see the sun, and he will contemplate him as he is.

Above ground: the freed man kneels by a still pool, studies his reflection, then looks up at the stars, and at last at the rising sun.

**SOCRATES (V.O.) (CONT'D)**

Imagine once more such a one coming suddenly out of the sun to be replaced in his old situation. Would he not have his eyes full of darkness? Men would say of him that up he went and down he came without his eyes. And if any one tried to loose another and lead him up to the light, let them only catch the offender, and they would put him to death.

BACK TO SCENE.

The courtyard is quiet. Socrates sits calmly in the lamplight.

**JULIAN (V.O.)**

In a few years, Athens will do exactly that. To him. And he's sitting here telling them how it ends.

**SOCRATES**

This entire allegory, dear Glaucon, you may append to what we said before. The prison-house is the world of sight, the light of the fire is the sun, and the journey upwards is the ascent of the soul into the world of mind. In the world of knowledge, the idea of good appears last of all, and is seen only with an effort.

Certain professors of education say they can put a knowledge into the soul which was not there before, like sight into blind eyes. But the power of learning exists in the soul already. It has only been turned in the wrong direction. Education is the art of turning it around.

Did you never observe the narrow intelligence flashing from the keen eye of a clever rogue? How eager he is, how clearly his paltry soul sees the way to his end. He is the reverse of blind, but his keen eyesight is forced into the service of evil, and he is mischievous in proportion to his cleverness.

**JULIAN (V.O.)**

Metis. In our booth he called it metis. Cleverness pointed the wrong way.

**SOCRATES**

And so those who have climbed into the light must not be allowed to stay there.

**GLAUCON**

But is not this unjust? Ought we to give them a worse life, when they might have a better?

**SOCRATES**

You have again forgotten, my friend, the intention of the lawgiver, who did not aim at making any one class in the city happy above the rest. The happiness was to be in the whole city. Wherefore each of you, when his turn comes, must go down to the general underground abode, and get the habit of seeing in the dark. When you have acquired it, you will see ten thousand times better than the inhabitants of the den, because you have seen the beautiful and just and good in their truth. And the city in which the rulers are most reluctant to govern is always the best and most quietly governed, and the city in which they are most eager, the worst.

**GLAUCON** (quietly)

Quite true.

Julian looks at the shadows on the wall: the company, large and flickering, and the bare stretch of plaster where he stands.

**JULIAN (V.O.)**

Must go down. Back to the den. Back to the lounge.

## Sequence Seven: The Tyrant and the Ring

**INT. HOUSE OF CEPHALUS – COURTYARD – BEFORE DAWN**

Most of the company is asleep. THRASYMACHUS snores on his couch; POLEMARCHUS dozes with his chin on his chest. Only GLAUCON and ADEIMANTUS are still awake, wrapped in their cloaks beside SOCRATES. The single lamp burns low.

**SOCRATES**

Every city decays, as every living thing decays. The best city falls to the lovers of honour, honour to the lovers of wealth, wealth to the lovers of liberty, and liberty to the tyrant. For the excess of liberty, whether in cities or individuals, seems only to pass into excess of slavery.

INSERT – THE DECLINE OF CITIES: four thrones descending a staircase into darkness. On the first, a soldier crowned with laurel. On the second, a man counting coins. On the third, a crowd, all shouting at once. On the last, a single figure with a wolf's shadow.

**SOCRATES (V.O.)**

The people have always some champion whom they set over them and nurse into greatness. This and no other is the root from which a tyrant springs: when he first appears above ground, he is a protector. But there is a tale, that he who has tasted the entrails of a single human victim is destined to become a wolf.

BACK TO SCENE.

**SOCRATES**

And he who is the real tyrant, whatever men may think, is the real slave. He has desires which he is utterly unable to satisfy, and has more wants than any one, and is truly poor, if you know how to inspect the whole soul of him. All his life long he is beset with fear.

**GLAUCON**

Then show us the whole soul, Socrates, as you promised.

**SOCRATES**

Model in your mind a many-headed monster, having a ring of heads of all manner of beasts, tame and wild. Then a second form, of a lion, and a third, of a man, each smaller than the one before. Join them, and let the three grow into one. Then fashion the outside of them into a single image, as of a man, so that he who sees only the outer hull may believe the beast to be a single human creature.

INSERT – THE BEAST WITHIN: the three creatures appear one by one in light: the writhing many-headed beast, the lion, the small man. They fold together, and a plain human skin closes over them. Inside, the beast's heads strain against the hull.

**SOCRATES (V.O.)**

To him who says that it is profitable to be unjust, let us reply: then it is profitable to feast the multitudinous monster and strengthen the lion, and to starve and weaken the man, who is dragged about at the mercy of the other two. But the just man gives the man within him the mastery. He watches over the many-headed monster like a good husbandman, fostering the gentle heads and preventing the wild ones from growing, and he makes the lion-heart his ally.

**JULIAN (V.O.)**

We built machines to feed the heads. Every one of them. A new head every quarter.

BACK TO SCENE.

**GLAUCON**

You speak of the city we have founded in words. I do not believe there is such a one anywhere on earth.

**SOCRATES**

In heaven there is laid up a pattern of it, which he who desires may behold, and beholding, may set his own house in order. But whether such a one exists, or ever will exist in fact, is no matter. He will live after the manner of that city, having nothing to do with any other.

Socrates turns in his chair until he faces the column in the corner, where nobody is standing. He speaks to it.

**SOCRATES (CONT'D)**

And so we have fulfilled the conditions of the argument. We have not called in the rewards and glories of justice. Justice in her own nature has been shown to be best for the soul in her own nature.

(slowly, to the empty air)

Let a man do what is just, whether he have the ring of Gyges or not, and even if in addition to the ring of Gyges he put on the helmet of Hades.

Glaucon follows his gaze to the column and sees nothing there. He looks back at Socrates, puzzled.

In the corner, Julian stands up. He looks at the ring for a long moment. Then he turns the bezel outward.

On the plastered wall, among the long shadows of the sleepers, a new shadow appears: Julian's own.

No one sees it but Socrates. The faintest smile.

**SOCRATES (CONT'D)**

And now, Glaucon, I will tell you a tale. Not one of the tales which Odysseus tells to the hero Alcinous, but the tale of a hero, Er the son of Armenius, a Pamphylian by birth.

INSERT – THE MYTH OF ER: a battlefield strewn with the dead. One body, unmarked by decay, is laid on a funeral pyre. On the twelfth day, as the torch is lowered, the man's eyes open.

**SOCRATES (V.O.)**

He was slain in battle, and on the twelfth day, as he was lying on the funeral pile, he returned to life and told them what he had seen in the other world. He went on a journey with a great company to a mysterious place, where there were two openings in the earth and two in the heaven above, and judges seated between them.

The images: a vast meadow; a column of light binding heaven and earth like a rainbow; the spindle of Necessity turning on her knees; the three Fates singing.

**SOCRATES (V.O.) (CONT'D)**

There, the souls were made to choose their next lives. A prophet scattered the lots among them, and laid out samples of lives on the ground: lives of every animal, and of men in every condition, tyrannies among them. And he said: "Virtue is free. The responsibility is with the chooser. God is justified."

A soul steps forward first and snatches up a glittering crown.

**SOCRATES (V.O.) (CONT'D)**

He who had the first choice came forward and in a moment chose the greatest tyranny. His mind had been darkened by folly, and he had not thought out the whole matter before he chose. When he saw what was in the lot, he began to beat his breast, and blamed chance and the gods and everything rather than himself. He had lived in a well-ordered city in his former life, but his virtue was a matter of habit only, and he had no philosophy.

Last of all, a weathered traveller searches through the lives left lying on the ground, and smiles at a plain one.

**SOCRATES (V.O.) (CONT'D)**

There came also the soul of Odysseus, and his lot was the last of them all. The recollection of former toils had disenchanted him of ambition, and he went about for a considerable time in search of the life of a private man who had no cares. He found it lying about, neglected by everybody else, and said that he would have done the same had his lot been first, and that he was delighted to have it.

Then the souls drank from the river of Unmindfulness, and each one as he drank forgot all things. Er alone was hindered from drinking. And in the morning, awaking suddenly, he found himself lying on the pyre.

BACK TO SCENE.

The sky above the courtyard is turning grey.

**SOCRATES**

And thus, Glaucon, the tale has been saved and has not perished, and will save us if we are obedient to the word spoken. Wherefore my counsel is, that we hold fast ever to the heavenly way and follow after justice and virtue always, considering that the soul is immortal and able to endure every sort of good and every sort of evil. Thus shall we live dear to one another and to the gods. And it shall be well with us, both in this life and in the pilgrimage of a thousand years which we have been describing.

A long silence. Adeimantus has fallen asleep. Glaucon's eyes close, his head drops against his brother's shoulder.

Socrates rises, steps carefully between the sleepers, and crosses the courtyard to Julian.

**SOCRATES** (quietly)

You came down to the Piraeus to see how they would celebrate the festival, which was a new thing.

**JULIAN**

You knew I was here. The whole night.

**SOCRATES**

A man who casts no shadow is easy to find, in a room full of shadows.

**JULIAN**

Glaucon was right about the ring. I could have taken anything in this house. Walked into the Assembly. Read every letter in Athens.

**SOCRATES**

And what did you take?

**JULIAN** (a beat)

Nothing. I listened.

**SOCRATES**

Then you have answered Glaucon better than I did all night, and in fewer words.

**JULIAN** (hesitating)

Socrates, I know how your story ends. The cave. The ones who try to lead the others up.

**SOCRATES** (raising a hand gently)

Then keep it to yourself. A man should not be told the end of his play while he is still acting in it.

(he glances back at the sleeping company, then at the wall of shadows)

In your lounge, you showed me your cave. Tonight I have shown you mine. It is the same den, Julian. Yours has better lamps.

He folds Julian's fingers over the ring, bezel outward.

**SOCRATES (CONT'D)**

Go down. Your city has need of a man who has seen the sun and come back with his eyes. And keep it turned this way. Let them see you.

**JULIAN**

Maintain the mirror.

**SOCRATES** (smiling)

Guard the water.

Julian turns the ring a full circle. The smell of salt and thyme gives way to smog and botanical vapor. The air shimmers, and he is gone.

Socrates stands alone among the sleepers. The first ray of sun crosses the courtyard and lights the garland on Cephalus's empty chair.

**INT. BOTANICAL LOUNGE – LOS ANGELES – DAWN**

The same grey-gold light through the blinds. Julian sits exactly where he sat, as though no time has passed.

The door opens. ARIA steps back in for a slate she left on the bench, and stops.

**ARIA**

You haven't moved.

Julian looks down at his hand. The brass ring is on his finger, bezel turned outward.

**JULIAN**

I went to a party in the Piraeus.

**ARIA** (half a laugh)

In ten minutes?

**JULIAN**

It lasted all night.

He stands, crosses to the window and pulls up the blinds. Sunlight floods the lounge. For the first time since he picked up the ring, Julian's shadow stretches long across the floor.

**JULIAN (CONT'D)**

Call Cleon. We're going back down.
